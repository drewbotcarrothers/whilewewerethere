Michelle is standing on a wet Paris sidewalk, autumn leaves stuck to the stone, shop shutters still down for the afternoon. It is 14 September 2013, and that is how our two weeks in France and Italy begin: not with a monument, but with the rain, and with the simple fact of having arrived.

This was years before Harrison, Sullivan, and Maeve were born. It was just the two of us, a camera roll that would grow to more than a thousand pictures, and a route that still makes sense when I line the files up by time.

## Paris, in the rain and then in grey light

That first evening the sky eased enough for us to walk. The photographs move toward Place de la Bastille and the July Column, and later down to the Seine. We found somewhere to eat, and the day ends in a handful of dim indoor pictures — the kind you take when you are tired and glad to be sitting down.

The next morning, 15 September, we were out at the Palace of Versailles. The Cour d'honneur is all brick and gold under a heavy sky, the kind of courtyard that makes you stop before you have even gone inside. We did go inside: the state rooms, the Hall of Mirrors, and then out into the gardens, formal and wide, with the cloud still hanging over the trees.

By late afternoon we were back in Paris and standing in front of the Eiffel Tower. Someone took a picture of the two of us together, a fence and a grey sky behind the ironwork. We went up after dark. From the structure itself the city drops away, and the lattice is lit a deep orange — closer and more tangled than it ever looks from the ground.

16 September is a long walk through the centre of the city. The morning photographs are at Palais-Royal and then at the Louvre, the glass pyramid sitting in the courtyard under a brighter sky than the day before. We went into the galleries — sarcophagi, paintings, the usual wonderful overload — and then kept walking: Place Vendôme, the Place de la Concorde, the Arc de Triomphe, and down the Champs-Élysées.

Late in the afternoon Michelle is on the Pont des Arts, looking out along the Seine, the railings still thick with padlocks. (Those locks were later taken down. In the photograph they are still there, row after row of them.)

On the morning of the 17th we took the Métro and walked up to Notre-Dame. This is the cathedral as it stood then, six years before the fire: the two towers, the rose window, the row of kings across the façade, all of it complete. We went inside as well. The last still photographs in Paris that day are from mid-afternoon. That evening we left for Florence.

## Florence, between the dome and the river

The first Florence photographs are from the morning of 18 September, under a softer, clouded sky. The cathedral is suddenly there at the end of a street: green and white marble, the red brick of Brunelleschi's dome. We walked the centre — the streets around the Duomo, the David replica in Piazza della Signoria, the Arno, the dim interiors of churches — and later through the market stalls at San Lorenzo, where the photographs fill up with produce and shop fronts. Evening is a dinner, close and warmly lit.

  <Image src={florenceDuomo} alt="The marble façade and red-brick dome of the Florence Cathedral, seen from close below on a cloudy day." widths={[480, 800, 1200, 1600]} sizes="(min-width: 40rem) 40rem, 100vw" />
  <figcaption>The Duomo, 18 September.</figcaption>

The 19th is another day on foot. Morning souvenir streets and porticoes, then the façade of Santa Croce, bright against a clear blue sky. In the afternoon we are along the Arno, walking toward the Ponte Vecchio, the river and the hills of the Oltrarno running beside us. Dinner, again, closes the day.

Two nights in Florence, and then the photographs turn south.

## South to Sorrento, and along the coast

On the morning of 20 September the view from a train window is fields, and then, unmistakably, the cone of Vesuvius. By early afternoon we are in Sorrento: narrow streets, the cliff road, and then the drop down to Marina Grande, a small harbour of coloured houses and boats, the sea opening out beyond the breakwater. That evening the same coast is a string of lights.

The 21st is a day on the Amalfi Coast. The early pictures are taken from a vehicle on the corniche, the road hanging above the water, and then the town of Positano comes into view the way it always does in photographs and still somehow does in person: houses stacked up the cliff, a church dome, the beach a narrow crescent at the bottom. We walked down to Spiaggia Grande, a field of red-and-white umbrellas and the ferry boats standing off the shore.

On the 22nd we spent the day at Pompeii. A bookshop at the entrance, then the ruins opening under a hard blue sky: temples, the Forum, streets of stone with Vesuvius sitting quietly at the end of them, a bakery, the amphitheatre, a vineyard with the mountain behind it, the small theatre. In the afternoon there are pictures of the sea again, the volcano still in the frame.

## A morning around Capri

We were at the port in Sorrento early on 23 September, and then on a boat. The photographs move along the cliffs of Capri, into the mouth of a sea cave, and then out to the Faraglioni — those three rocks in deep water, one of them opened by an arch, which look exactly as every painting has already told you they will, and are still worth the trip.

<figure>
  <Image src={faraglioni} alt="The Faraglioni rocks off Capri, one of them pierced by a natural arch, standing in dark blue water under a clear sky." widths={[480, 800, 1200, 1600]} sizes="(min-width: 40rem) 40rem, 100vw" />
  <figcaption>The Faraglioni, from the water, 23 September.</figcaption>
</figure>

By mid-afternoon we are high above the coast again, looking down on Positano: the tiled dome, the beach packed with umbrellas, the town poured down the hill into the bay. That is the photograph at the top of this story.

The 24th is almost empty in the camera roll — a single picture in a souvenir shop, a shelf of Roman figurines, and then nothing. I think of it as a travel day, pointing us toward Rome.

## Rome, and a quiet morning in the square

On the morning of 25 September the Colosseum is simply there at the end of the street, with the sign for Via del Colosseo in the foreground and the traffic moving around it. We spent the rest of the day walking the centre of the city: the Trevi Fountain in full sun, the Pantheon, Piazza Navona, Castel Sant'Angelo, a market stall of spices and bags of pasta, the Forum in the late light, and dinner as the streets got dark.

We were in the Forum again on the 26th, in stronger sun, at the Temple of Antoninus and Faustina — the inscription still readable along the entablature, the columns standing against a bare blue sky — and among the pines and brick of the rest of the site.

On the 27th we arrived early at St. Peter's Square. The colonnades, the obelisk, the façade and the dome are almost empty in the first light. We went inside, under the baldachin, and then the photographs thin out. The last frame of the trip, on the 28th, is at Fiumicino: an airport, and the ordinary end of a long way from that wet sidewalk in Paris.

## Looking back

Lined up by time, the trip is a straight path: a rainy street in Paris, a grey day at Versailles and a night in the ironwork of the tower, the cathedral as it then was, the dome in Florence, a harbour in Sorrento, the coast at Positano, a street in Pompeii aimed at a volcano, the rocks off Capri, and a quiet morning in front of St. Peter's.

I did not need GPS to retrace it. The clocks on the photographs were enough. It remains a map of a stretch of time when it was only the two of us, and a route I would still be glad to walk again.
