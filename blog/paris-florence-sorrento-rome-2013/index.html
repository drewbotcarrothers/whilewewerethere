From Paris to Rome: An Early Fall Trip Overview

Michelle and I spent two weeks walking from a rainy first afternoon in Paris to Florence, the Amalfi Coast, Capri, Pompeii, and Rome—a route we can still follow stop by stop through our photographs.

Paris to Rome · Europe

Watch our channel on YouTube for the films that pair with these stories.

Michelle is standing on a wet Paris sidewalk, early fall leaves stuck to the stone, shop shutters still down for the afternoon. That is how our two weeks in France and Italy begin: not with a monument, but with the rain, and with the simple fact of having arrived.

It was just the two of us, a camera roll that would grow to more than a thousand pictures, and a route that still makes sense when I line the stops up in order.

Paris, in the rain and then in grey light

That first evening the sky eased enough for us to walk. We headed toward Place de la Bastille and the July Column, and later down to the Seine. We found somewhere to eat, and the day ends in a handful of dim indoor pictures—the kind you take when you are tired and glad to be sitting down.

The next morning we were out at the Palace of Versailles. The Cour d’honneur is all brick and gold under a heavy sky, the kind of courtyard that makes you stop before you have even gone inside. We did go inside: the state rooms, the Hall of Mirrors, and then out into the gardens, formal and wide, with the cloud still hanging over the trees.

By late afternoon we were back in Paris and standing in front of the Eiffel Tower. Someone took a picture of the two of us together, a fence and a grey sky behind the ironwork. We went up after dark. From the structure itself the city drops away, and the lattice is lit a deep orange—closer and more tangled than it ever looks from the ground.

Day three was a long walk through the centre of the city. The morning began at Palais-Royal and then at the Louvre, the glass pyramid sitting in the courtyard under a brighter sky than the day before. We went into the galleries—sarcophagi, paintings, the usual wonderful overload—and then kept walking: Place Vendôme, the Place de la Concorde, the Arc de Triomphe, and down the Champs-Élysées.

Late in the afternoon Michelle stopped on the Pont des Arts, looking out along the Seine with the railings still thick with padlocks. (Those locks were later taken down, so the photograph is a snapshot of how the bridge looked then.)

On our fourth morning we took the Métro and walked up to Notre-Dame—the two towers, the rose window, and the row of kings across the façade before the fire and restoration. We went inside as well, spent the early afternoon around central Paris, and flew to Florence later that day. Read the full Paris 4-day itinerary for Versailles, the Eiffel Tower, and walking the Right Bank.

Florence, between the dome and the river

Our first morning in Florence began under a softer, clouded sky. The cathedral is suddenly there at the end of a street: green and white marble, the red brick of Brunelleschi’s dome. We walked the centre—the streets around the Duomo, the David replica in Piazza della Signoria, the Arno, the dim interiors of churches—and later through the market stalls at San Lorenzo, piled with produce and shop fronts. Evening was a dinner, close and warmly lit.

The second day was another long walk on foot: morning porticoes, then the façade of Santa Croce bright against a clear blue sky. In the afternoon we walked along the Arno toward the Ponte Vecchio, the river and the hills of the Oltrarno running beside us. Dinner closed the day.

Two nights in Florence, and then the route turns south. See the Florence 2-day itinerary for Santa Croce, Santissima Annunziata, and the Ponte Vecchio walk.

South to Sorrento, and along the coast

We took a high-speed train from Florence to Naples and transferred onto the Circumvesuviana train down the bay. From the window heading south, flat green fields gave way to the unmistakable cone of Vesuvius. By early afternoon we were in Sorrento: narrow streets, the cliff road, and then the drop down to Marina Grande, a small harbour of coloured houses and boats with the sea opening out beyond the breakwater. That evening the same coast was a string of lights.

The next day we caught a SITA bus onto the Amalfi Coast. Looking out from the coastal corniche hanging above the water, the town of Positano comes into view the way it always does in photographs and still somehow does in person: houses stacked up the cliff, a church dome, and a narrow crescent of beach at the bottom. We walked down to Spiaggia Grande, past rows of red-and-white umbrellas and the ferry boats standing off the shore.

After that came a day trip to Pompeii on the Circumvesuviana train—very convenient from Sorrento—where we followed a self-guided tour with a Rick Steves audio guide: the entrance bookshop, then the ruins opening under a hard blue sky—temples, the Forum, streets of stone with Vesuvius sitting quietly at the end of them, a bakery, the amphitheatre, a vineyard with the mountain behind it, and the small theatre. In the afternoon the sea came back into view with the volcano still on the horizon. See our full Pompeii day trip from Sorrento guide for the Forum, Amphitheatre, and current ticketing rules.

A morning around Capri

Early the next morning we were down at Marina Piccola in Sorrento to catch the public ferry—the boat ride along the coast was breathtaking. Off Capri we saw the white limestone cliffs, a sea cave along the shore, and the Faraglioni—those three rocks in deep water, one of them opened by an arch, which look exactly as every painting has already told you they will, and are still worth the trip.

By mid-afternoon we were high above the coast again, looking down on Positano: the tiled dome, the beach packed with umbrellas, and the town poured down the hill into the bay. That is the photograph at the top of this story.

The next day we took the train back to Rome. Read the full Sorrento and Amalfi Coast itinerary for Marina Grande, Positano, and Capri.

Rome, and a quiet morning in the square

On our first morning in Rome the Colosseum was simply there at the end of the street, with the sign for Via del Colosseo in the foreground and the traffic moving around it. We spent the rest of the day walking the centre of the city: the Trevi Fountain in full sun, the Pantheon, Piazza Navona, Castel Sant’Angelo, a market stall of spices and bags of pasta, the Forum in the late light, and dinner as the streets got dark.

The second day took us back into the Forum in stronger sun, at the Temple of Antoninus and Faustina—the inscription still readable along the entablature, the columns standing against a bare blue sky—and among the pines and brick arches of Palatine Hill.

On our third morning in Rome we arrived early at St. Peter’s Square. The colonnades, the obelisk, the façade, and the dome were almost empty in the first light. We went inside under the baldachin, and the trip ended the following day at Fiumicino Airport—an ordinary finish a long way from that wet sidewalk in Paris. See the Rome and Vatican City 3-day itinerary for the Pantheon, Castel Sant’Angelo, Palatine Hill, and current booking notes.

Looking back

Lined up in order, the trip is a straight path: a rainy street in Paris, a grey day at Versailles and a night in the ironwork of the tower, the cathedral, the dome in Florence, a harbour in Sorrento, the coast at Positano, a street in Pompeii aimed at a volcano, the rocks off Capri, and a quiet morning in front of St. Peter’s.

I did not need GPS to retrace it. The sequence of the photographs was enough. It remains a map of two weeks with just the two of us, and a route I would still be glad to walk again.
